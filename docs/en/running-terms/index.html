Running glossary

The unfamiliar words you meet as a new runner, in plain language. Type a term to filter instantly.

Times and races

PB · PRPersonal Best · Personal Record
Your best-ever time. When you set a new one, you "got a PB".
Sub-3 · sub-4
Finishing a marathon in under 3 or 4 hours. Sub-4 means up to 3:59:59. Used the same way for the half and 10K. Goal pace chart →
Net time · gun time
Gun time runs from the start signal; net time runs from the moment you cross the start line to the finish. Starting from a back corral, the two can differ by several minutes. Use net time to judge your fitness.
Race bibBIB
The number you pin to your chest. The timing chip is often on the back, so don't fold or crumple it.
DNS · DNF
DNS means you entered but didn't start (Did Not Start); DNF means you started but didn't finish (Did Not Finish).
Cutoff
The time limit set by the race. For a marathon it's around 5–6 hours depending on the race, and some set cutoffs at checkpoints too. Check the race guide.
PacerPacemaker
A runner who holds a steady pace for a target time. They carry a balloon or flag showing the time.
Even pace
Running the same speed from start to finish. Make splits →
Negative split
Running the second half faster than the first. When the second half is slower, it's a positive split.
Hitting the wall
Around 30 km in a marathon, your legs suddenly feel heavy and your pace drops. Running out of stored carbohydrate is considered the main cause.
Tapering
The 1–3 weeks before a race when you cut training volume to shed fatigue.
Carb loading
Eating plenty of carbohydrate for 2–3 days before a race to fill your muscles' fuel stores.

Training

Pace
The time it takes to run 1 km. A 5 minutes 30 seconds pace is written 5:30/km. Pace converter →
Easy run
Running at a comfortable pace where you can talk with the person next to you. It makes up most of your weekly running. Calculate my easy pace →
Recovery run
A very light, short run the day after a hard session or race to shake off fatigue.
LSDLong Slow Distance
A long run at a slow pace. The weekend long run is often called this.
Steady run
A continuous run at a set pace without breaks.
Tempo run · threshold runTempo run
A continuous run of about 20 minutes at a "comfortably hard" pace. Builds the ability to hold a fast pace for longer.
IntervalsInterval
Alternating fast running and rest. For example, run 1 km fast and rest 2–3 minutes, five times.
Build-up run
A run that starts slow and gets a little faster each section.
Fartlek
Swedish for "speed play": mixing fast and slow running without a fixed structure.
Strides
Short accelerations of 80–100 m for about 15–20 seconds, fast but relaxed. Add a few at the end of an easy run.
Warm-up · cool-down
Easy running before and after the main session to warm up and cool down.
Cross-training
Building fitness with exercise other than running, like cycling or swimming. Used to maintain fitness when injured.
Weekly distance
The total distance you run in a week. Also called mileage. Weekly plan →
Cadence
Steps per minute. Watches show it as spm.

Body and fitness

VO2max
Maximal oxygen uptake: the most oxygen your body can use per minute during exercise. The standard measure of aerobic fitness. My fitness age →
Runjisu
An aerobic fitness index worked backward from a race result. From one result it gives training paces and predicted times at other distances. Calculate my Runjisu →
Lactate threshold
The point where, as intensity rises, fatigue byproducts suddenly start to build up. Speeds near it are your threshold pace.
Running economy
How little energy you use to run at a given speed. With the same VO2max, better economy means faster times.
Max heart rate · heart-rate zones
Max heart rate is the most times your heart can beat in a minute; heart-rate zones divide intensity by percentage of it. Calculate heart-rate zones →
Glycogen
Carbohydrate stored in your muscles and liver; the main fuel for running.
Delayed-onset muscle sorenessDOMS
Muscle soreness that shows up a day or two after exercise. Worse after downhill running or a harder-than-usual workout.
Overuse injury
An injury that comes not from a single accident but from repeated load building up. Most running injuries fall into this group. Running injury guide →
Runner's high
The good, light feeling that comes during a long run.

Gear

Heel drop
The height difference between a shoe's heel and forefoot. Given in mm; also called offset.
Stack height
The thickness of the sole under your foot.
Carbon-plated shoes
Racing shoes with a carbon-fiber plate and highly responsive foam. Also called super shoes. Running shoe types →
Stability shoes
Shoes with structure that limits the foot from rolling too far inward on landing.
Pronation
The natural slight inward roll of the foot on landing.
Energy gel
A carbohydrate supplement you take while running, mostly for half marathons and longer.
